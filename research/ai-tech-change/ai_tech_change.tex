\begin{abstract}
\noindent This note uses \citet{kiley2004} (working paper \citealp{kiley2000}) as a
blueprint for analyzing AI-driven technical change. The blueprint has one capital
stock and two pairings of production and capital-accumulation technologies, each with a
closed form. (i) CES production with an investment adjustment cost that vanishes on the
balanced growth path gives $Q=1$ and closed forms for the interest rate, the wage, the
labor share and value/output on that path. (ii) CES production with a CES
capital-accumulation function of the same elasticity,
$K'^{\rho}=\bar A^{\rho}[(1-\delta)K^{\rho}+\delta I^{\rho}]$, gives an exact closed form for the
\emph{entire} equilibrium path when the intertemporal elasticity of substitution equals
the production elasticity. The saving rate is then constant,
$s=[B\bar A^\rho\delta a_K/(1-B\bar A^\rho(1-\delta))]^{\e}$, and capital follows a linear law
of motion in $K^\rho$. The Cobb-Douglas case, $K'=\bar AK^{1-\delta}I^{\delta}$ with log utility, is
the $\e\to1$ limit. There value/output is constant and the real interest rate and real
wage follow from a log-linear law of motion. I add AI capital as a third input. It combines with the capital-labor bundle
through a CES aggregator, and its technology improves either disembodied (a factor
$\Gamma$ on the installed stock) or embodied (a falling price $P_A$ of new units, as in
\citealp{ghk1997}). Under pairing (i), the balanced growth path has closed forms for any
elasticity, an AK regime for substitutes and a Baumol limit for complements. Under
pairing (ii), with all three inputs sharing one elasticity, the transition paths of $r$
and $W$ after any AI shock are closed form. Saving rates depend only on the technology
path, not on the state.
Embodied and disembodied progress with the same long-run effect raise
$\sum_t\ln(1+r_{t+1})$ by exactly the same amount, the long-run log output gain, and
differ only in timing. A gradual (S-curve) disembodied shock raises $r$ sooner and more.
A surprise permanent step does the reverse: embodied progress raises the ex-ante real
rate more, because disembodied progress delivers its first gain as a windfall. With
complements, a disembodied step even lowers $r$.
\end{abstract}

\section{The blueprint: two closed-form pairings with one capital stock}

\citet{kiley2000} studies a representative-agent economy (notation as in that paper).
The household maximizes
\begin{equation*}
U = E_t\sum_{j=t}^{\infty} B^{j-t}\,\frac{[C(j)^b L(j)^{1-b}]^{1-s}}{1-s},
\qquad L = 1-N, \tag{2.1}
\end{equation*}
and holds claims to capital, $C(t)+Q(t)K(t+1)=(Q(t)+D(t))K(t)+W(t)N(t)$. The
calibration (Table~1 of the working paper) is $s=1$ (log utility), $b=0.25$, $a=0.282$,
$d=0.066$, $r=0.075$, with $c=15$ for ``moderate'' adjustment costs. Let $\rho\equiv 1/B-1$
be the rate of time preference and $\theta\equiv 1-b(1-s)$ the inverse intertemporal
elasticity (footnote~11 of the paper). With constant hours on a balanced growth path,
$1+r=(1+g)^\theta/B$.

I could not obtain the published article's text. The working paper has the
Cobb-Douglas, quadratic-cost case. The pairings below follow the published
article's structure as the author describes it. CES production pairs with an
adjustment-cost function, and with a CES investment/accumulation function of the same
elasticity, which gives an exact solution with a constant saving rate. The Cobb-Douglas
production/accumulation pair is the special case $\e=1$. I derive the closed forms here.

\subsection{Pairing (i): CES production and adjustment costs}

Production and the adjustment cost are
\begin{gather*}
Y=\Big[\alpha^{1/\e}K^{\frac{\e-1}{\e}}+(1-\alpha)^{1/\e}(ZN)^{\frac{\e-1}{\e}}\Big]^{\frac{\e}{\e-1}}
-\Psi[I/K]K, \tag{2.7$'$}\\
\Psi[I/K]K=0.5\,c\,(I/K-x)^2K,\qquad x=g+d,
\end{gather*}
with $K(t+1)=I(t)+(1-d)K(t)$. (The paper writes the cost as $C[\cdot]$; I use $\Psi$ to
avoid a clash with consumption.) Then $Q=1+\Psi'$ (2.11), and the Euler equation is
(2.12) with the CES marginal product. The cost vanishes at $I/K=g+d$, so on any balanced
growth path $Q=1$ and $F_K=r+d\equiv R$. Writing $F_K=(\alpha Y/K)^{1/\e}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac KY=\alpha R^{-\e},\quad \text{capital share}=\alpha R^{1-\e},\quad
\frac WZ=\Big[\frac{1-\alpha R^{1-\e}}{1-\alpha}\Big]^{\frac1{1-\e}},\quad
\frac VY=(1+g)\,\alpha R^{-\e}\approx\frac{\alpha}{(r+d)^{\e}} .
\label{eq:ces1}
\end{equation}
For $\e=1$ this is (3.2), $V/Y=a/(r+d)$. The elasticity of value/output to the
required return is $-\e$. The paper's argument is that production mutes the valuation effect of a
fall in $r$, relative to the Gordon model, because dividends fall as investment
rises. That muting is strongest when capital is a poor substitute for labor. Table~\ref{tab:cesr}
repeats the paper's Section~3 experiment ($r$ from 7.5 to 4 percent, $g=0$), normalizing
$\alpha$ so the capital share is 0.282 initially. Pairing (i) gives closed forms on the
balanced growth path for any $\e$. Away from it, CES makes capital's output elasticity
vary with $K/ZN$, and the dynamics need numerical methods.

\subsection{Pairing (ii): Cobb-Douglas production and Cobb-Douglas accumulation}

Now let production be Cobb-Douglas without separate adjustment costs, and let capital
accumulate through a Cobb-Douglas technology
\citep{lucasprescott1971,hercowitzsampson1991}:
\begin{equation}
Y=K^{\alpha}(ZN)^{1-\alpha},\qquad K(t+1)=\bar A\,K(t)^{1-\delta}I(t)^{\delta},\qquad C+I=Y.
\label{eq:cdcd1}
\end{equation}
Inverting the accumulation function shows that it \emph{is} an adjustment-cost
technology. The investment needed to reach $K'$ is $I=K\,(K'/\bar AK)^{1/\delta}$, which is
convex in the growth of capital. The price of installed capital is
\begin{equation*}
Q=\frac{\partial I}{\partial K'}=\frac{I}{\delta K'},\qquad
\frac{\partial\ln(I/K)}{\partial\ln Q}=\frac1{1-\delta}.
\end{equation*}
To first order around a path with $I/K=g+d$, (\ref{eq:cdcd1}) is the usual
$K'=(1-d)K+I$ when $\delta=(g+d)/(1+g)$ and $\bar A=(1+g)^{1-\delta}\delta^{-\delta}$, and then $Q=1$
on that path. The quadratic cost gives an elasticity of $I/K$ to $Q$ of $1/(cx)$. Matching
the two gives $c=(1-\delta)/x$. With the paper's $g=0$ and $d=0.066$, the CD technology
implies $c\approx14$, essentially the paper's ``moderate'' $c=15$.

\begin{proposition}[Exact solution, one capital stock]\label{prop:cdcd1}
With $s=1$ (so $U=b\ln C+(1-b)\ln L$) and any path for $Z$, anticipated or not, the
equilibrium of (\ref{eq:cdcd1}) is
\begin{gather*}
\frac IY=s_I\equiv\frac{B\delta\alpha}{1-B(1-\delta)},\qquad
N=\frac{b(1-\alpha)}{b(1-\alpha)+(1-b)(1-s_I)},\qquad
\frac VY=\frac{QK'}{Y}=\frac{s_I}{\delta}=\frac{\alpha}{\rho+\delta},\\
1+r(t+1)=(1+\rho)\frac{Y(t+1)}{Y(t)},\qquad W=\frac{(1-\alpha)Y}{N},\\
\ln K(t+1)=[1-\delta(1-\alpha)]\ln K(t)+\delta(1-\alpha)\ln Z(t)+\text{const}.
\end{gather*}
\end{proposition}
\emph{Proof sketch.} Guess $V(K)=\kappa\ln K+$ terms in $Z$. The first-order condition
$1/C=B\kappa\delta/I$ and the envelope condition $\kappa=\alpha Y/C+B\kappa(1-\delta)$
give constant $I/Y$ and $C/Y$. The labor condition $(1-b)C/(bL)=(1-\alpha)Y/N$ then fixes
$N$. Since $C/Y$ is constant, $U_C(t)/U_C(t+1)=C(t+1)/C(t)=Y(t+1)/Y(t)$, which gives $r$.
Finally, $V=QK'=I/\delta$.

Pairing (ii) makes the whole transition closed form, not just the balanced growth path.
It also changes one of the paper's conclusions. Value/output is $\alpha/(\rho+\delta)$, the
paper's (3.2) with the time-preference rate $\rho$ in place of $r$. It is \emph{independent of
growth}: faster growth lowers $K/Y$ but raises $Q$ above one by exactly the
offsetting amount. Under pairing (i), faster growth lowers $V/Y$. With $g=0$, both pairings
agree that a fall in the required return (a rise in $B$) raises $V/Y$ by the same
proportion as $\rho+\delta$ falls.

\subsection{Pairing (ii), general case: CES production and CES accumulation}

The Cobb-Douglas pair generalizes. Let $\rho\equiv(\e-1)/\e$ and
\begin{equation}
Y^\rho=a_KK^\rho+a_L(ZN)^\rho,\qquad
K(t+1)^\rho=\bar A^\rho\big[(1-\delta)K(t)^\rho+\delta I(t)^\rho\big],\qquad
U=\sum_t B^t\frac{C_t^{\rho}}{\rho},
\label{eq:cescesd1}
\end{equation}
with hours fixed. In the paper's preferences, fixed hours make utility a power of $C$
with exponent $b(1-s)$, so the last condition is $b(1-s)=\rho$. Equivalently, the
intertemporal elasticity of substitution $1/\theta$ equals the production elasticity
$\e$. The accumulation technology is CES in old capital and new investment, with the
same elasticity as production. As in the Cobb-Douglas case, inverting it gives a convex
adjustment-cost function, $I=\{[(K'/\bar A)^\rho-(1-\delta)K^\rho]/\delta\}^{1/\rho}$, and
$Q=\partial I/\partial K'=(I/K')^{1/\e}/(\bar A^\rho\delta)$.

\begin{proposition}[Exact solution, CES pair]\label{prop:cesces1}
For any path of $Z$, anticipated or not, the value function is
$V_t(K)=\kappa K^\rho/\rho+h_t$, and
\begin{gather*}
\frac IY=s=\Lambda^{\e},\qquad
\Lambda\equiv\frac{B\bar A^\rho\delta a_K}{1-B\bar A^\rho(1-\delta)},\qquad
\kappa=\frac{a_K(1-s)^{-1/\e}}{1-B\bar A^\rho(1-\delta)},\\
K(t+1)^\rho=\bar A^\rho\big[(1-\delta+\delta a_Ks^\rho)K(t)^\rho+\delta a_Ls^\rho(Z(t)N)^\rho\big],\\
1+r(t+1)=\frac1B\Big(\frac{Y(t+1)}{Y(t)}\Big)^{1/\e},\qquad
W=a_LZ\Big(\frac{Y}{ZN}\Big)^{1/\e},\\
\frac{WN}{Y}=a_L\Big(\frac{ZN}{Y}\Big)^{\rho},\qquad
\frac VY=\frac{s^{1/\e}}{\bar A^\rho\delta}\Big(\frac{K'}{Y}\Big)^{\rho}.
\end{gather*}
As $\e\to1$, $s\to B\delta\alpha/(1-B(1-\delta))$ and the law of motion becomes log-linear:
Proposition~\ref{prop:cdcd1}.
\end{proposition}
\emph{Proof sketch.} With the guess for $V_{t+1}$, the Bellman objective is
$(Y-I)^\rho/\rho+B\kappa\bar A^\rho[(1-\delta)K^\rho+\delta I^\rho]/\rho$. The first-order condition
$C^{\rho-1}=B\kappa\bar A^\rho\delta I^{\rho-1}$ makes $I/C$, and therefore $I/Y$, a constant
$\phi^{\e}$ with $\phi=B\kappa\bar A^\rho\delta$. Substituting back, the maximized objective is
$(1+\phi^\e)^{1/\e}Y^\rho/\rho+B\kappa\bar A^\rho(1-\delta)K^\rho/\rho$. This is linear in $K^\rho$
and $(ZN)^\rho$, so the guess reproduces itself with
$\kappa=(1+\phi^\e)^{1/\e}a_K+B\bar A^\rho(1-\delta)\kappa$. Solving the two equations gives
$s=\phi^\e/(1+\phi^\e)=\Lambda^\e$. The interest rate follows from $C\propto Y$ and the
household Euler equation.

Three features matter for what follows. First, $\rho$ appears in all three places
(production, accumulation and utility). This is what makes the problem linear in
$K^\rho$, and it is why CES production with Cobb-Douglas accumulation, or with log
utility, is not exact. Second, the saving rate is $\Lambda^\e$, so
$d\ln s=\e\,d\ln\Lambda$. Its response to the return on saving (through $B$, $\delta$ or $a_K$)
is $\e$ times the Cobb-Douglas response. Third, the normalization
$\delta=x^{1/\e}/(x^{1/\e}+1-d)$ and $\bar A^\rho=(1+g)^{-1/\e}(x^{1/\e}+1-d)$, with $x=g+d$,
makes the CES accumulation function agree to first order with $K'=(1-d)K+I$ on the
balanced growth path. Then $Q=1$ there, and the path coincides with pairing (i)'s. This
generalizes the Cobb-Douglas normalization.

\section{The model with AI capital}

This section sets out the three-input model with pairing (i) technologies.
Sections~\ref{sec:cdcd} and~\ref{sec:cesces} replace the accumulation technology
with pairing (ii): first Cobb-Douglas, then the general CES pair.

\paragraph{Technology.} The traditional bundle and gross output are
\begin{equation}
X = K^a (ZN)^{1-a},\qquad
F = \Big[\omega^{1/\e} X^{\frac{\e-1}{\e}} + (1-\omega)^{1/\e} M^{\frac{\e-1}{\e}}\Big]^{\frac{\e}{\e-1}},
\qquad M=\Gamma A .
\end{equation}
Here $A$ is AI capital measured in efficiency units and $M$ is effective AI. With $\e<1$,
AI complements the capital-labor bundle; with $\e>1$, it substitutes for it. The
distribution weights carry the exponent $1/\e$, so $1-\omega$ is the AI income share
when the effective cost of AI is one, and the $\e\to1$ limit is
$F=X^\omega M^{1-\omega}/[\omega^\omega(1-\omega)^{1-\omega}]$.

\paragraph{Two kinds of AI progress.}
\emph{Disembodied}: $\Gamma$ grows at rate $\gamma_\Gamma$ and raises the productivity
of the entire installed stock. \emph{Embodied}: new AI efficiency units cost $P_A$ in
output, and $P_A$ falls at rate $\gamma_q$, with $P_A(t)/P_A(t+1)=1+\gamma_q$. This is the
investment-specific technical change of \citet{ghk1997}: a unit of output buys
$1/P_A$ efficiency units, but units already installed do not improve.

\paragraph{Accumulation and adjustment costs.} Following (2.7) for both stocks,
\begin{gather}
K(t+1)=I_K(t)+(1-d_K)K(t),\qquad A(t+1)=I_A(t)+(1-d_A)A(t),\\
Y = F - 0.5c_K(I_K/K-x_K)^2K - P_A\,0.5c_A(I_A/A-x_A)^2A,
\end{gather}
and $C + I_K + P_A I_A = Y$. AI adjustment costs are scaled by $P_A$, so they stay in
proportion to AI spending as $P_A$ falls, which is the appendix specification with its
own investment price. The parameters $x_K,x_A$ equal steady-state investment rates.

\paragraph{Optimality.} Labor demand is $W=F_X(1-a)X/N$. Investment gives
$Q_K = 1+c_K(I_K/K-x_K)$ and $Q_A = P_A[1+c_A(I_A/A-x_A)]$. Write
$m_{t+1}=B\,U_C(t+1)/U_C(t)=1/(1+r_{t+1})$. The Euler equations are
\begin{align}
Q_K(t) &= m_{t+1}\big\{F_K(t+1) - \Psi_K(t+1) + \Psi_K'(t+1)\,i_K(t+1) + (1-d_K)Q_K(t+1)\big\},\\
Q_A(t) &= m_{t+1}\big\{\Gamma(t+1)F_M(t+1) + P_A(t+1)[\Psi_A'\,i_A-\Psi_A](t+1) + (1-d_A)Q_A(t+1)\big\},
\end{align}
where $i=I/K$ or $I/A$ and $\Psi_j(i)=0.5c_j(i-x_j)^2$. The first is (2.12). The second
is its AI analogue. The household conditions are
$(1-b)C/(bL)=W$ and $U_C(t)=B\,E_t[U_C(t+1)(1+r(t+1))]$.
Firm value is $V(t)=Q_K(t)K(t+1)+Q_A(t)A(t+1)$; by \citet{hayashi1982} this equals the present value of
dividends $D=Y-WN-I_K-P_AI_A$.

\section{Pairing (i) with AI: closed forms on the balanced growth path}

Define the \emph{effective cost of AI}, the rental price of one unit of $M$,
\begin{equation}
\lambda \equiv \frac{P_A\,u_A}{\Gamma},\qquad u_A = (1+r)(1+\gamma_q) - (1-d_A)\;\approx\; r+d_A+\gamma_q .
\end{equation}
The user cost $u_A$ contains an obsolescence term $\gamma_q$: a unit bought today loses
value as newer, cheaper units arrive. The user cost of traditional capital is
$R_K=r+d_K$. Let $\theta \equiv 1-b(1-s)$ be the inverse intertemporal elasticity
(footnote 11 of the paper).

\begin{proposition}[Balanced growth]\label{prop:bgp}
Suppose either (i) $\Gamma/P_A$ is constant, for any $\e$, or (ii) $\e=1$ with
$\Gamma$ growing at $\gamma_\Gamma$ and $P_A$ falling at $\gamma_q$. On the balanced
growth path with growth $g$, $Q_K=1$, $Q_A=P_A$, hours are constant, and
\begin{align}
1+r &= (1+g)^{\theta}/B, \label{eq:r}\\
s_M &= (1-\omega)\,\lambda^{1-\e} \quad\text{(AI share of income)}, \label{eq:sM}\\
\text{labor share } \tfrac{WN}{Y} &= (1-a)(1-s_M), \label{eq:labsh}\\
P_X &= \big[(1-s_M)/\omega\big]^{\frac{1}{1-\e}} \;\; (\e\neq1), \qquad P_X=\lambda^{-\frac{1-\omega}{\omega}}\;\;(\e=1),\label{eq:PX}\\
\frac{W}{Z} &= (1-a)\Big[P_X\Big(\frac{a}{r+d_K}\Big)^{a}\Big]^{\frac{1}{1-a}}, \label{eq:w}\\
\frac{K}{Y} &= \frac{a(1-s_M)}{r+d_K},\qquad \frac{P_AA}{Y}=\frac{s_M}{u_A},\\
\frac{V}{Y} &= (1+g)\Big[\frac{K}{Y}+(1+\gamma_q)\frac{P_AA}{Y}\Big]
\approx \frac{a(1-s_M)}{r+d_K}+\frac{s_M}{r+d_A+\gamma_q}, \label{eq:VY}\\
N &= \frac{b(1-a)(1-s_M)}{b(1-a)(1-s_M)+(1-b)\,C/Y},\\
\frac CY &= 1-(g+d_K)\frac KY-\big[(1+g)(1+\gamma_q)-1+d_A\big]\frac{P_AA}{Y}.
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Derivation.} With $Q$'s constant and adjustment costs zero, the Euler equations
give $F_K=r+d_K$ and $\Gamma F_M=P_Au_A$, the latter because $Q_A=P_A$ falls at rate
$\gamma_q$ (a capital loss). Competitive pricing makes unit cost equal one,
$1=[\omega P_X^{1-\e}+(1-\omega)\lambda^{1-\e}]^{1/(1-\e)}$, where $P_X$ is the shadow
price of the bundle. This gives (\ref{eq:sM}) and (\ref{eq:PX}). The Cobb-Douglas
bundle's cost function $P_X = (R_K/a)^a(W/[Z(1-a)])^{1-a}$, inverted, gives
(\ref{eq:w}). The interest rate (\ref{eq:r}) is the household Euler equation with
constant hours. Market value follows from $Q=1$ and the timing of $V$.

Equation (\ref{eq:VY}) is the AI generalization of (3.2): each capital stock is valued at
its income share over its user cost. A compact way to read the wage formula is
\begin{equation}
\ln\frac{W}{Z} = \text{const} + \frac{\ln(1-s_M)}{(1-\e)(1-a)} - \frac{a}{1-a}\ln(r+d_K).
\label{eq:wlog}
\end{equation}
AI moves the real wage through two channels only: the share of income it absorbs
(scaled by $1/(1-\e)$) and the interest rate. When $\e>1$, a rising AI share raises the
wage level even as it lowers the labor share. When $\e<1$, cheaper AI shrinks its share
and the wage gain is bounded.

\subsection{Cobb-Douglas: an exact balanced growth path with permanent AI progress}

\begin{proposition}[$\e=1$]
With $\Gamma$ growing at $\gamma_\Gamma$ and $P_A$ falling at $\gamma_q$,
\begin{equation}
\ln(1+g) = \ln(1+g_Z) + \frac{1-\omega}{(1-a)\,\omega}\big[\ln(1+\gamma_\Gamma)+\ln(1+\gamma_q)\big],
\end{equation}
AI capital in efficiency units grows at $(1+g)(1+\gamma_q)$, $s_M=1-\omega$, the labor
share is $(1-a)\omega$, and $r$ follows from (\ref{eq:r}). The wage grows at $g$, and its
level relative to $Z$ is
$W/Z=(1-a)\big[\lambda^{-\frac{1-\omega}{\omega}}(a/R_K)^a\big]^{\frac{1}{1-a}}$, with
$\lambda=P_Au_A/\Gamma$ falling at $(1+\gamma_\Gamma)(1+\gamma_q)$.
\end{proposition}

Disembodied and embodied progress at the same rate deliver the same growth rate and
the same real interest rate. They differ in levels, because embodied progress raises
the AI user cost by the obsolescence term. Relative to disembodied progress at the same
rate, the wage level is lower by
$\frac{1-\omega}{(1-a)\omega}\ln[u_A^{\text{emb}}/u_A^{\text{dis}}]$ log points,
$P_AA/Y$ is lower by the factor $u_A^{\text{dis}}/u_A^{\text{emb}}$, and so is the AI
contribution to $V/Y$. Table~\ref{tab:bgp} shows this.

As in \citet{kiley2000}, faster growth raises $r$ one-for-one with $\theta=1$ and
\emph{lowers} $K/Y$ and $V/Y$. AI-driven growth does not mechanically raise valuation
ratios. Embodied progress lowers $V/Y$ further because AI assets depreciate economically
as their price falls.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Substitutes ($\e>1$)}{Substitutes (epsilon > 1)}: the AK regime}

\begin{proposition}[$\e>1$]
Let $\bar A\equiv(1-\omega)^{1/(\e-1)}$ and $r_Z=(1+g_Z)^\theta/B-1$. With $\Gamma/P_A$
constant, an interior balanced growth path exists if and only if
$\lambda(r_Z)=P_A(r_Z+d_A)/\Gamma>\bar A$, i.e. $s_M<1$. Otherwise the economy converges
to an AK path in which $F\to\bar A\,\Gamma A$ and
\begin{equation}
r = \bar A\,\Gamma/P_A - d_A,\qquad 1+g = [B(1+r)]^{1/\theta} > 1+g_Z .
\end{equation}
On this path the bundle's shadow price grows at
$\ln G_P = \frac{(1-a)(\ln(1+g)-\ln(1+g_Z))}{\e(1-a)+a}$, so
\begin{equation}
\Delta\ln W = \ln(1+g_Z) + \frac{\ln(1+g)-\ln(1+g_Z)}{\e(1-a)+a},\qquad
\Delta\ln\text{(labor share)} = -(\e-1)\ln G_P .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

In the AK regime, the real wage grows faster than $Z$ but more slowly than output, so
the labor share declines toward zero while workers become richer. The interest rate is
pinned by the AI rental rate, not by $g_Z$. If $\Gamma/P_A$ keeps rising, so does the AK
productivity $\bar A\Gamma/P_A$, and $r$ and $g$ rise without bound. This is the
explosive-growth knife edge. At the baseline calibration with $\e=1.5$, the threshold is
reached only after $\Gamma/P_A$ rises about 1{,}100-fold from its initial level, so the
20-fold shock studied below stays in the interior regime.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Complements ($\e<1$)}{Complements (epsilon < 1)}: the Baumol limit}

\begin{proposition}[$\e<1$]
If $\Gamma/P_A\to\infty$, then $\lambda\to0$, $s_M=(1-\omega)\lambda^{1-\e}\to0$, $g\to g_Z$,
$r\to r_Z$, the labor share $\to 1-a$, $P_X\to\omega^{-1/(1-\e)}$, and
\begin{equation}
\frac{W_\infty}{W_0} = (1-s_{M,0})^{-\frac{1}{(1-\e)(1-a)}} .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

AI becomes so cheap that it stops being a bottleneck. Output is limited by the
traditional bundle, and the total wage gain from AI is capped by its initial income
share. With $s_{M,0}=0.03$, $\e=0.5$ and $a=0.282$, the cap is 8.9 percent.

\section{Pairing (ii) with AI: exact dynamics}\label{sec:cdcd}

Set $\e=1$, so that $F=K^{\alpha_K}(ZN)^{\alpha_N}(\Gamma A)^{\alpha_A}$ (up to a constant) with
$\alpha_K=a\omega$, $\alpha_N=(1-a)\omega$, $\alpha_A=1-\omega$. Replace both linear
accumulation equations and both adjustment costs with Cobb-Douglas accumulation:
\begin{equation}
K(t+1)=\bar A_K K(t)^{1-\delta_K}I_K(t)^{\delta_K},\qquad
A(t+1)=\bar A_A A(t)^{1-\delta_A}\big[I_A(t)/P_A(t)\big]^{\delta_A},\qquad
C+I_K+I_A=Y .
\label{eq:cdcdAI}
\end{equation}
Here $I_A$ is AI spending in output units, so $1/P_A$ is the number of efficiency units
one unit of output buys. Embodied progress (falling $P_A$) raises only the
productivity of new AI investment. Disembodied progress (rising $\Gamma$) raises the
productivity of the whole stock.

\begin{proposition}[Exact solution with AI]\label{prop:cdcdAI}
With $s=1$ and any paths of $Z$, $\Gamma$ and $P_A$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Saving rates are constant and separate by asset:
$\displaystyle \frac{I_j}{Y}=s_j=\frac{B\delta_j\alpha_j}{1-B(1-\delta_j)}$ for $j=K,A$.
Hours are $N=b\alpha_N/[b\alpha_N+(1-b)(1-s_K-s_A)]$.
\item Market value is $V=Q_KK'+Q_AA'=I_K/\delta_K+I_A/\delta_A$, so
$\displaystyle\frac VY=\frac{\alpha_K}{\rho+\delta_K}+\frac{\alpha_A}{\rho+\delta_A}$,
constant along every path.
\item $\ln(1+r_{t+1})=\ln(1+\rho)+\Delta\ln Y_{t+1}$. The real wage is $W=\alpha_NY/N$, so
$\ln W$ moves one-for-one with $\ln Y$, and the labor share is constant at $\alpha_N$.
\item With $x_t=(\ln K_t,\ln A_t)'$, $\alpha=(\alpha_K,\alpha_A)'$ and lowercase letters for logs,
\begin{gather*}
y_t=\alpha'x_t+\alpha_A\gamma_t+\alpha_N(z_t+\ln N),\\
x_{t+1}=Mx_t+\begin{pmatrix}\delta_K\\ \delta_A\end{pmatrix}\big(\alpha_A\gamma_t+\alpha_Nz_t\big)
-\begin{pmatrix}0\\ \delta_A\end{pmatrix}p_{A,t}+\text{const},\\
M=\begin{pmatrix}1-\delta_K(1-\alpha_K)&\delta_K\alpha_A\\ \delta_A\alpha_K&1-\delta_A(1-\alpha_A)\end{pmatrix}.
\end{gather*}
The eigenvalues of $M$ are closed form ($\mu_{1,2}=\tfrac12[\mathrm{tr}M\pm((\mathrm{tr}\,M)^2-4\det M)^{1/2}]$).
With $\delta_K=\delta_A=\delta$ they are $1-\delta$ and $1-\delta\alpha_N$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The real interest rate and the real wage are therefore linear functionals of the AI
technology paths:
\begin{equation}
\ln\frac{1+r_{t+1}}{1+r^*}=\alpha_A\Delta\gamma_{t+1}+\alpha'(x_{t+1}-x_t)\quad\text{(deviations from the no-shock path)}.
\label{eq:rpath}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Same area, different timing]\label{prop:area}
Let $\ln\Gamma$ rise by $\Delta_\Gamma$ and $\ln(1/P_A)$ by $\Delta_q$, along any paths.
Then output rises in the long run by $\ln Y_\infty-\ln Y_\infty^*=\frac{\alpha_A}{\alpha_N}(\Delta_\Gamma+\Delta_q)$.
The cumulative interest-rate deviation $\sum_{t\ge0}\big[\ln(1+r_{t+1})-\ln(1+r^*)\big]$
equals the same amount, less any jump in $\ln Y_0$ (a disembodied shock that arrives at
$t=0$ raises output on impact, and that windfall is not part of any ex-ante rate). The wage path is the output path. Embodied and disembodied progress
of equal size move $r$ by the same total and differ only in timing.
\end{proposition}

For a surprise permanent step of size $\Delta$ at $t=0$, (\ref{eq:rpath}) gives
$\ln\frac{1+r_{t+1}}{1+r^*}=\alpha'M^tf$ with
\begin{equation*}
f_{\text{dis}}=\alpha_A\Delta\begin{pmatrix}\delta_K\\ \delta_A\end{pmatrix},\qquad
f_{\text{emb}}=\Delta\begin{pmatrix}0\\ \delta_A\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\frac{\alpha'f_{\text{emb}}}{\alpha'f_{\text{dis}}}=\frac{\delta_A}{\alpha_K\delta_K+\alpha_A\delta_A}.
\end{equation*}
A disembodied step raises output immediately by $\alpha_A\Delta$. That gain is a
\emph{windfall} (an unexpected realized return), so it never shows up in the ex-ante
real rate, and only the accumulation that follows raises $r$. An embodied step raises
nothing on impact. All of its effect comes through investment, so the ex-ante real rate
jumps by more: 9.6 versus 7.8 percent in the calibration below. When the same total
improvement arrives gradually and is anticipated (the S-curve), the direct term
$\alpha_A\Delta\gamma_{t+1}$ in (\ref{eq:rpath}) raises expected growth under
disembodied progress. Then $r$ rises sooner and more under disembodied progress, and the
embodied response is a distributed lag of it with the same area.

\paragraph{Calibration and comparison with pairing (i).} I set
$\delta_j=(g_Z+d_j)/(1+g_Z)$, which gives $\delta_K=0.080$ and $\delta_A=0.212$. The initial
balanced growth path is then identical to the one in Section~\ref{sec:trans} (the saving
rates equal $(g+d_j)\times$capital/output exactly). The eigenvalues of $M$ are 0.943 and
0.794. Figure~\ref{fig:cdcd} and Table~\ref{tab:cdcd} compare the exact solution with
the quadratic-cost model solved numerically, using the curvature that gives the same
$Q$-elasticity ($c_K=11.4$, $c_A=3.7$). The two are almost indistinguishable. The exact model is
therefore a reliable analytical guide to the quadratic-cost dynamics when $\e=1$. Value/output is
constant in the exact model and nearly constant in the quadratic one.

\paragraph{CES with Cobb-Douglas accumulation is not exact.} The Cobb-Douglas pair
depends on each input's output elasticity being constant. With $\e\neq1$ production but
Cobb-Douglas accumulation, AI's share $(1-\omega)\lambda^{1-\e}$ moves with the effective cost
of AI, and so do the optimal saving rates. The script solves this mixed economy
numerically (the disembodied S-curve). With $\e=1.5$, $I_A/Y$ rises from 0.030 to 0.105,
and with $\e=0.5$ it falls from 0.021 to 0.005. The exact solution for $\e\neq1$ requires
the CES accumulation function of the next section.

\section{Pairing (ii) with AI, general case: CES production and CES accumulation}\label{sec:cesces}

To keep the problem linear in $X^\rho$ for every capital stock, all three inputs share
the elasticity $\e$, and each capital stock accumulates through a CES function of the
same elasticity:
\begin{gather}
Y^\rho=a_KK^\rho+a_L(ZN)^\rho+a_A(\Gamma A)^\rho,\nonumber\\
K'^\rho=\bar A_K^\rho\big[(1-\delta_K)K^\rho+\delta_KI_K^\rho\big],\qquad
A'^\rho=\bar A_A^\rho\big[(1-\delta_A)A^\rho+\delta_A(I_A/P_A)^\rho\big],
\label{eq:cesAI}
\end{gather}
with $U=\sum_tB^tC_t^\rho/\rho$ and hours fixed. AI is now equally substitutable for capital
and labor. The nested form of Section~2, with a Cobb-Douglas $K$--$L$ bundle inside the CES,
is exact only when $\e=1$, because $X^\rho=K^{a\rho}(ZN)^{(1-a)\rho}$ is not linear in $K^\rho$.

\begin{proposition}[Exact solution with AI, CES pair]\label{prop:cesAI}
For any paths of $Z$, $\Gamma$ and $P_A$, the value function is
$V_t=(\kappa_{K,t}K^\rho+\kappa_{A,t}A^\rho)/\rho+h_t$. Define
$\phi_{K,t}=B\kappa_{K,t+1}\bar A_K^\rho\delta_K$ and
$\phi_{A,t}=B\kappa_{A,t+1}\bar A_A^\rho\delta_AP_{A,t}^{-\rho}$. Then
\begin{gather*}
\frac{I_{j,t}}{Y_t}=s_{j,t}=\frac{\phi_{j,t}^{\e}}{1+\phi_{K,t}^{\e}+\phi_{A,t}^{\e}},\qquad
\Omega_t=\big(1+\phi_{K,t}^{\e}+\phi_{A,t}^{\e}\big)^{1/\e},\\
\kappa_{K,t}=\Omega_ta_K+B\bar A_K^\rho(1-\delta_K)\kappa_{K,t+1},\qquad
\kappa_{A,t}=\Omega_ta_A\Gamma_t^\rho+B\bar A_A^\rho(1-\delta_A)\kappa_{A,t+1}.
\end{gather*}
Saving rates are functions of time alone. They are found by recursing backward from the
final steady state and do not depend on the capital stocks. They do not depend on $Z$
at all, and they are constant whenever $\Gamma$ and $P_A$ are. Prices are
$1+r_{t+1}=B^{-1}(C_{t+1}/C_t)^{1/\e}$ and $W=a_LZ(Y/ZN)^{1/\e}$. The labor share is
$a_L(ZN/Y)^\rho$ and the AI share is $a_A(\Gamma A/Y)^\rho$.
\end{proposition}

Two features differ from the $\e=1$ case. First, when $\e\neq1$, news about AI
changes saving rates on announcement, because $\kappa_{A,t}$ looks forward through
$\Gamma^\rho$ and $P_A^{-\rho}$. With $\e=1$, saving rates ignore technology entirely.
Second, the direction of that response depends on $\e$. With substitutes, cheaper
future AI raises the value of AI capital, and $I_A/Y$ rises. With complements, cheaper
AI means less AI spending is needed, and $I_A/Y$ falls.

\paragraph{Results.} The calibration has the same initial shares, interest rate and
investment rates as before, with the IES set equal to $\e$ (Table~\ref{tab:cesexact},
Figure~\ref{fig:cesexact}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{S-curve.} For every $\e$, disembodied progress raises $r$ sooner and higher
than embodied progress (peaks of 8.5 vs 8.3 percent for $\e=0.5$ and 9.4 vs 9.1 percent
for $\e=1.5$). Long-run wage gains are 6.8, 13.8 and 38 percent, close to the
closed forms of pairing (i).
\item \emph{Surprise step.} Embodied progress raises the ex-ante real rate more for every $\e$.
With complements, a disembodied step \emph{lowers} $r$, to 7.4 percent. The AI saving rate drops at
once from 0.023 to 0.005, so consumption jumps up and then grows more slowly. With
substitutes, the step raises $r$ to 10.3 (disembodied) or 12.3 percent (embodied), because the
AI investment boom pushes consumption growth up.
\item \emph{Exactness depends on IES $=\e$.} With log utility and $\e=0.5$, the generic
solver shows that $I_K/Y$ moves between 0.157 and 0.163 after a labor-technology
shock, so it is no longer constant. With IES $=\e$, the same shock leaves both saving rates
exactly unchanged.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Pairing (i) with AI: transitions for embodied versus disembodied shocks}\label{sec:trans}

The script \texttt{ai\_model.py} solves the perfect-foresight path with a Newton method
on the stacked equilibrium conditions, with all variables detrended by $Z$. The
calibration is the paper's: $a=0.282$, $b=0.25$, $s=1$, $d_K=0.066$, $c_K=15$, initial
$r=0.075$. Trend growth is $g_Z=0.015$, where the paper used 0 for convenience. The AI
block has $d_A=0.20$, $c_A=15$, an initial AI income share of 3 percent, and $\Gamma_0$
normalized so that $\lambda_0=1$, which makes the initial path identical for all $\e$.
At $t=0$ agents learn that $\Gamma/P_A$ will rise 20-fold along a logistic S-curve
centered on year 10. The rise comes either through $\Gamma$ (disembodied) or through
$P_A$ (embodied). Both shocks have the same initial and final balanced growth paths,
which Proposition~\ref{prop:bgp} gives in closed form, so all of the difference lies in
the transition.

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The real interest rate rises temporarily in both cases, and more under
disembodied progress.} Disembodied progress raises the productivity of AI already in
place, so output and expected consumption growth rise as the S-curve unfolds, and $r$
rises with them. The peak is 8.6 percent in the Cobb-Douglas case and 9.7 percent with
$\e=1.5$. Embodied progress pays off only through new vintages, and accumulation is slowed by
adjustment costs. The rise in $r$ is therefore smaller (8.2 and 8.9 percent at the peak),
comes later and lasts longer. Under complements the rise is smallest, because AI's share
collapses as it becomes cheap. In every case $r$ returns to $r_Z$: a one-time
level shift has no long-run effect on $r$. Only a permanent change in the growth rate
does (Table~\ref{tab:bgp}).
\item \emph{The real wage rises in all cases, by an amount set by $\e$.} The
long-run gain is 6.8 percent with complements (close to the 8.9 percent Baumol cap), 13.8
percent with Cobb-Douglas, and 37 percent with substitutes, all from (\ref{eq:wlog}).
Under embodied progress the wage lags by roughly five years: ten years in, the wage is up
1.5 percent under embodied progress versus 4--6 percent under disembodied progress.
\item \emph{Labor share.} The gross labor share moves only through $s_M$. It rises with
complements and falls with substitutes, from 69.7 to 62.2 percent at $\e=1.5$. In the
Cobb-Douglas case it is constant gross of adjustment costs. The temporary rise under
embodied progress reflects the adjustment costs of the AI investment boom, which reduce
net output.
\item \emph{Valuation.} Embodied progress generates an AI investment boom with
$Q_A/P_A>1$ and a temporary rise in $V/Y$. Disembodied progress in the
complements case \emph{lowers} $V/Y$ in the transition, as in the paper's growth
experiment. In the long run, $V/Y$ follows (\ref{eq:VY}) and rises only when the
AI share rises ($\e>1$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Verification}

The script checks every closed form against the equilibrium conditions directly. It
builds level paths, without detrending, from the closed forms and evaluates the
production function, both Euler equations, the labor condition and the household
Euler equation. It then compares $V$ with the present value of dividends. It does this
for the Cobb-Douglas path with permanent embodied and/or disembodied progress and for
CES steady states with $\e\in\{0.5,1.5\}$. It also checks the Baumol limit, the
consistency of the AK threshold, and that every transition converges to the
closed-form new path. Table~\ref{tab:checks} reports the largest error for each check.

\section{Extensions}
\begin{itemize}
\item Calibrating $\e$, $d_A$ and $\gamma_q$ to AI and software investment data, and
comparing the implied paths for $r$ with long-term real yields.
\item Letting AI substitute for labor within the bundle (nested CES with task
structure) to separate wage-level from labor-share effects.
\item Adding an equity premium as in Section~3 of the paper, by moving $B$, to separate
growth-driven from discount-rate-driven valuation.
\end{itemize}
